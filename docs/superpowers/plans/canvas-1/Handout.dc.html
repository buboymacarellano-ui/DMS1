Design review

Three directions for
the DMS interface

The system works. It does not look like it works. These are three ways to modernise the interface without changing how anyone does their job — same screens, same steps, same data. Only the appearance changes.

Each direction is shown on the same screen, the Stores dashboard, with the same figures. That way the only difference you are judging is the design itself.

What we need from you

Pick one letter — A, B or C. You are choosing a look, not a final screen: once a direction is agreed, every screen in the system gets rebuilt to match it, one department at a time, starting with the login page and the Stores portal.

Nothing changes for staff until each department is signed off separately.

A

Refined corporate

The current look, brought up to date

Keeps the company blue and the general shape of what staff already use, then fixes what makes it feel old: hard square corners become soft ones, flat grey boxes gain gentle shadow and depth, and cramped rows get room to breathe. Numbers grow larger with a small arrow showing whether they are up or down on yesterday.

Anyone using the system today would recognise it immediately. Nothing needs re-learning — buttons stay where they are, the same words label the same things. It simply looks like software maintained this year rather than a decade ago.

Best for

Keeping every branch comfortable while still looking current.

The trade-off

It is the safe choice. Modern and tidy, but no one will call it striking.

Retraining needed

None.

B

Dense operations console

Built for the people who live in it all day

A working tool rather than a website. Dark background, a permanent menu down the left, and rows packed tight so far more fits on one screen without scrolling. Figures are set in a fixed-width typeface so columns of pesos line up exactly — easier to scan for the odd one out, and easier to spot a variance.

This is the direction that respects a cashier's or a parts clerk's time most: fewer clicks, less scrolling, more visible at once. It is also the least welcoming to someone who only opens the system occasionally.

Best for

Desk staff on the system for hours — parts, finance, cashier control.

The trade-off

Reads as technical. A dark screen is an unusual choice in a bright shop, and occasional users may find it cold.

Retraining needed

Light — the menu moves from the top to the left.

Direction B — Stores dashboard

C

Warm and roomy

Easiest on a tablet and on the eye

The friendliest of the three. Warm off-white instead of grey, generously rounded cards, and noticeably larger text throughout. Every button and row is sized for a finger rather than a mouse, so it works properly on a tablet carried around the shop floor. The dashboard opens by telling you what needs attention today rather than listing everything at once.

This is the direction that looks least like traditional business software. It is the most pleasant to use occasionally, and the one that shows the least information per screen.

Best for

Tablets on the shop floor, and staff who use the system in short bursts.

The trade-off

Least information per screen. Desk staff working through long lists will scroll more.

Retraining needed

Light — same screens, larger and rearranged.

What happens after you choose

The chosen direction is drawn up properly — every shared piece of the interface, plus the login page and the Stores portal in full. Those two go live first, on their own, so any problem affects one small part of the system rather than all of it. Each remaining department follows the same way, and each one can be reversed on its own if a branch is unhappy.

Nothing about how the system works is changing. Same screens, same steps, same records.
